\chapter{Proposed Method}

The task at hand can be described as target area search and navigation in an unknown environment. 
It is an active research field, even though the specific task varies. In fact, different authors have considered using MARL solutions
to target localization, path planning and source seeking problems, which are all related to the problem at hand 
but with different objectives. Granadeno et al. \cite{granadeno2025multisource} focuses on multi-source plume tracing,
a task where a team of agents is required to find the source of a chemical plume in a 3D environment. The authors 
used a MARL approach, with cooperative agents ad CTDE paradigm. They found that their approach
outperformed other methods based on non-action dependent networks, and with a higher efficiency. Moreover,
they found out that by increasing the number of cooperative agents, the success rate improves. 
Other works, such as \cite{tan2024adaptiveqpso}, \cite{ma2024improvedqpso}, \cite{saeed2024energyefficient}, \cite{meerza2019qpso}, \cite{li2022qualityoriented} and \cite{li2025qlmopso}, 
tackle the problem of path planning using hybrid approaches based on Q-learning and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), the former being another RL algorithm 
based on action-value function and the latter being a population-based optimization algorithm that simulates the social behavior of birds and fishes, i.e.
the velocity vector of each particle is updated based on its own best position and the best position of the swarm. It is highly effective with large swarms,
where the effect of the social component is more pronounced and lets the swarm converge faster and more efficiently.
Finally, other works such as \cite{li2024multiauvsearch}, \cite{li2025multiauvmoving} and \cite{hou2024uavswarmsearch} explore the use of MARL 
approaches in the task of target search and localization in unknown environments, in the shortest possible time. Here, bayesian approaches and algorithms like MADDPG or QMIX are
used, always with the CTDE paradigm, and the authors found that their approaches outperform other methods like Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) and Deep Q-Networks (DQN), with higher efficiency.

The novelty of this work, in fact,
is the use of MARL algorithms to search a specific target area, specified by some parameters that in 
this case are the turbidity and the salinity, and the creation of a realistic 3D environment with Oceananigans. To do so, IPPO \cite{dewitt2020ippo} and MAPPO \cite{yu2022mappo} have been 
chosen as first approaches to solve the problem, given their popularity and effectiveness in multi-agent scenarios. The following sections will describe 
them in detail. 

\section{IPPO}

Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO) is the multi-agent version of the popular Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm \cite{schulman2017ppo}, 
an on-policy RL algorithm that uses a clipped surrogate objective to update the policy. In IPPO, each agent is used to learn a common policy, so the agents share
its parameters. It is like having $N$ independent agents each with the same PPO-trained policy on a single-agent scenario,
but the use of shared parameters helps having a more efficient sampling since in an episode multiple gradients are computed and used from different parts of the environment.

The problem is formulated as a Decentralized Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (Dec-POMDP), in a cooperative setting. Formally, it is defined by a tuple $\langle \mathcal{S},
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{O},\mathcal{R},\mathcal{P},n,\gamma\rangle$, where $\mathcal{S}$ is the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ is the shared action space for each agent $i$, $\mathcal{O}$ is the 
local observation space (each $o_i$ is the local observation for agent $i$ at global state $s$), $\mathcal{P}(s'|s,A)$ is the transition probability from $s$ to $s'$ given the joint action 
$A$, $\mathcal{R}(s,A)$ is the shared reward function and $\gamma$ is the discount factor. 
Agents use a policy $\pi_{\theta}(a_i|o_i)$ to produce an action from the local observation and jointly optimize the discounted accumulated reward $\mathcal{J}(\theta) = \mathbf{E}_{A^t,s^t}\left[\sum_t\gamma^t R(s^t,A^t)\right]$.

In IPPO, a variant of the advantage function is used, where each agent learns a local observation based critic $V_{\phi}(z^a_t)$ parameterised by $\phi$ using \textit{Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)}. For each agent
$a$, the relative advantage function is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    A^a_t = \sum_{l=0}^{h}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta^a_{t+l}
\end{equation}
where $\delta^a_t = r^a_t + \gamma V_{\phi}(z^a_{t+1}) - V_{\phi}(z^a_t)$ is the temporal difference error, $\gamma$ is the discount factor and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter that controls the bias-variance trade-off of the advantage estimates.
The final policy loss for each agent is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}^a(\theta) = \mathbf{E}_{z^a_t,u^a_t}\left[\min\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(u^a_t|z^a_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(u^a_t|z^a_t)}A^a_t,\text{ clip }\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(u^a_t|z^a_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(u^a_t|z^a_t)},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A^a_t\right)\right]
\end{equation}

The chosen policies are two simple MLPs with two hidden layers of 256 units for the actor and 128 for the critic. Moreover, multiple 
environments are stepped in parallel, so that each global step is composed of $n$ agents in $E$ environments. For a total number 
of timesteps, the relative number of iterations is given by $\frac{\text{total\_timesteps}}{n\cdot E\cdot \text{max\_steps}}$. In each iteration,
a number of updates given by \textit{update\_epochs} is performed, with a minibatch size equal to $\frac{\text{max\_steps}}{\text{num\_minibatches}}$. 
Observations and returns are normalized, and the algorithm stops when the Kullback Leibler (KL) divergence between the current policy and the old one
is higher than \textit{target\_kl}. The full procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ippo}. A binary mask $m^a_t$ excludes from the loss the
steps of agents that have already terminated their episode, so that frozen agents do not contribute spurious gradients. The used hyperparameters are in Tables~\ref{tab:env_params} and \ref{tab:ippo_params}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{IPPO}
\label{alg:ippo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialize $\theta,\phi$ using Orthogonal initialization
\While{$step\leq step_{max}$}
    \State $\theta_{old} \gets \theta$ \Comment{freeze the behaviour policy}
    \For{$t = 0,\dots,T-1$} \Comment{rollout phase}
        \ForAll{agents $a$ in each environment}
            \State sample $a^i_t \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a^i_t|o^i_t)$ and store $\log\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a^i_t|o^i_t)$ and $a^i_t$
        \EndFor
        \State step the environments and observe $r^i_t$, $o^i_{t+1}$ and the termination flags $d^a_{t+1}$
        \State store the transition together with the mask $m^a_t = 1 - d^a_t$
    \EndFor
    \For{epoch $k = 1,\dots,K$} \Comment{update phase}
        \For{$t = T-1,\dots,0$} \Comment{precompute adv with current critic each epoch}
            \State $\delta^i_t \gets r^i_t + \gamma\,(1-d^i_{t+1})\,V_{\phi}(o^i_{t+1}) - V_{\phi}(o^i_t)$
            \State $Adv^i_t \gets \delta^i_t + \gamma\lambda\,(1-d^i_{t+1})\,Adv^i_{t+1}$, \quad $\hat{V}^i_t \gets Adv^i_t + V_{\phi}(o^i_t)$
        \EndFor
        \ForAll{minibatches $b \subset \mathcal{B}$ of size $|\mathcal{B}|/M$} \Comment{gradient computation}
            \State normalize the advantages $A^a_t$ over $b$ to zero mean and unit variance
            \State $\rho^i_t \gets \pi_\theta(a^i_t|\tilde{o}^i_t) \,/\, \pi_{\theta_{old}}(a^i_t|\tilde{o}^i_t)$
            \State $\mathcal{L} \gets
                    -\langle \min(\rho Adv,\, \mathrm{clip}(\rho,1\!\pm\!\epsilon)Adv)\rangle_m
                    + c_v \langle \tfrac{1}{2}(V_\theta - \hat{V})^2 \rangle_m
                    - c_e \langle \mathcal{H}[\pi_\theta] \rangle_m$
            \State update $(\theta,\phi)$ by gradient descent on $\mathcal{L}$
                    (Adam, gradient norm clipped)
        \EndFor
        \If{$\widehat{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\Vert\pi_{\theta}\big) > \kappa$}
            \State \textbf{break} \Comment{early stopping on excessive policy change}
        \EndIf
    \EndFor
\EndWhile
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{MAPPO}

The policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the value function $V_{\phi}$ are two separate neural networks, where the latter is used for variance reduction and only utilized during training. 
Hence, as explained in \cite{yu2022mappo}, it can be centralized and given access to the global state $s$. This cretes the CTDE paradigm, where the policy is decentralized and only uses local observations, while the critic is centralized and uses global information.
This is the only thing that differentiates MAPPO from IPPO, as exposed in Algorithm~\ref{alg:mappo}. The hyperparameters are the same as in Table~\ref{tab:ippo_params}.

        \State step the environments and observe $r^i_t$, $o^i_{t+1}$ and the termination flags $d^a_{t+1}$
        \State store the transition together with the mask $m^a_t = 1 - d^a_t$

\section{Agent State}

In a RL framework, the agent state is the information that the agent receives from the environment at each timestep. 
It must be carefully designed to provide the agent with enough information to make it able to learn the optimal policy in 
the given environment. Moreover, it must take into consideration the real world constraints of the problem,
such as the limited sensing capabilities of the agents. In this work, there is a \textit{local state}, which is the information
that each agent receives from the environment considering the fact that underwater vehicles don't know their global position,
and a \textit{global state}, which considers the global position of the agents and is used in the centralized critic of MAPPO. The local state is composed of the following information:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}$, the components of the currents at the agent's position;
    \item $\frac{\partial S}{\partial u},\frac{\partial S}{\partial v},\frac{\partial S}{\partial w}$, the components of the salinity gradient at the agent's position. These could theoretically be computed by the agent using some sensors, so they were included in the state;
    \item $S - S*$, the difference between the salinity computed at the agent's position and the target salinity;
    \item $\Tau - \Tau*$, the difference between the turbidity computed at the agent's position and the target turbidity;
    \item $z$, the depth of the agent, which is important to know since the turbidity is a function of it and it is measurable by the agent using a pressure sensor;
    \item \textit{history} of the last $k$ $(\delta x,\delta y,\delta z, S - S*, \Tau - \Tau*)$ tuples, where $\delta x,\delta y,\delta z$ are the relative displacements of the agent in the last $k$ steps, that could be measured in real world from the agent by knowing its initial positions and the actions it does. This is important to give the agent a sense of direction and speed, since it doesn't know its global position.
\end{itemize}

I have found out that this is the minimum information that the agent needs to learn an optimal policy for this task,
and specifically the history is crucial, since it helps achieving a higher success rate. The reason for this is probably
that the agent can make a sort of global map of where it is going and where it has already been, improving the search efficiency
and effectiveness. Moreover, I added a \textit{communication} flag that simulates the exchange of information between agents
in the whole domain, so that when it is active the agents can share their local displacement and target differences, i.e. in addition 
to the local state, each agent receives $N-1$ tuples of $(\delta x,\delta y,\delta z, S - S*, \Tau - \Tau*)$ from the other agents. 
This is important to improve the cooperation between agents and to avoid them searching in the same areas. As shown below, it helps when agents 
start in the same position.

The global state, instead, adds on top of the local state the global horizontal position of the agent, i.e. $x,y$. This is used in the centralized critic of MAPPO,
which is only used during training and so can be feed with global information the agent does not have during execution. The final state space is a vector of $11$ elements, against 
the $9 + 5\cdot k (+ 5\cdot (N-1))$ elements of the local state. 

\section{Reward Function}

The reward function is crucial in RL, since it is the only feedback that the agent receives from the environment.
Usually, a sparse reward is used, so that the agent receives a negative unitary reward at each timestep and a positive 
reward when it reaches the goal. However, this approach is not idea in most cases, since the environment could be very 
large and complex, and the agent may reach the goal only few times during training, making it almost impossible a 
successful learning in that context. For that reason, a dense reward function is used in such cases. It is simply the 
construction of a function that reward the agent for going toward the goal, so that the closer it is to it, the higer 
the reward it receives. The difficulty is to find a correct function so that the agent is not 
incentivized to stay in a local minimum, i.e. a position where it receives a high reward but is not the goal, and has no
incentives to deviate from that point. The idea is to use a \textit{potential} value $\phi(s)$, associated with each state $s$,
and add to the reward of a transition the difference of potentials \cite{ng1999policy}. Thus, a shaping reward function $\mathcal{F}: \mathcal{S}\times \mathcal{A}\times \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$ is potential-based if there exists $\phi: \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$ such that:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{F}(s,a,s') = \gamma\phi(s') - \phi(s)
\end{equation}
This is the concept of \textit{potential reward shaping}, and is used in this work. If $\mathcal{F}$ is a potential-based function, then 
every optimal policy in $M' = \langle \mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R} + \mathcal{F},\gamma\rangle$ is also an optimal policy in $M = \langle \mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma\rangle$ and vice versa.

Thus, the reward function used in this work is based on the distance of the agent from the closest point of the target area:
\begin{equation}
    \phi(s) = 10\cdot \left(1 - \frac{d}{diag}\right)
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the distance of the agent from the closest point of the target area and $diag$ is the diagonal of the domain, calculated as $\sqrt{L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2}$.
Moreover, at success the agent receives a bonus of $20.0$, which makes the final reward function:
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \begin{cases}
        \gamma\phi(s') - \phi(s) & \text{if not success} \\
        20.0 & \text{if success}
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\section{Results}

The trained policies were eight in total, given by the combination of the two algorithms (IPPO and MAPPO), with and without communication, and with two different numbers of agents (2 and 4). 
Training of the policies with 2 agents was done for 10M timesteps and took 3-5 hours each, Figure~\ref{fig:training_2N} shows the episodic return, length and success rate during training.
\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/fig7_training_curves_N2.pdf}
    \caption{Training curves for 2 agent policies.}
    \label{fig:training_2N}
\end{figure}
As can be seen, the agents start acting randomly, with a low success rate and a high episode length. As the training progresses,
the agents learn to behave correctly and start reaching higher returns and success rates, with lower episode lengths. One small caveat
is that since the target zone is sampled randomly at each episode, it may be so large that the agents just need to learn to go in the 
target depth to reach the goal, creating a scenario where pure spawn luck can lead to success. This is reflected much more in the 
training curves of the 4 agent policies, where the number of agents is higher and so the chance of one of them spawning close to 
the target zone is much higher. In fact, Figure~\ref{fig:training_4N} shows flat curves, as if agents already start behaving optimally,
but in reality the majority of the success is due to spawn luck. This, unfortunately, is an intrinsic limitation of the task, 
since the target is an area and not a single point, and trying to avoid this by sampling a smaller region would introduce bias 
in the training and create a false optimal policy. Training of the policies with 4 agents was done for 20M timesteps, with minibatch size of 48 and took 6-8 hours each.

    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/fig7_training_curves_N4.pdf}
    \caption{Training curves for 4 agent policies.}
    \label{fig:training_4N}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory_2N} shows the trajectories developed during an episode by the IPPO policy without communication and using 2 agents. 
From a horizontal point of view, they are as one might expect, showing willingness to reach the target area and fighting the currents in doing so.
However, from a vertical point of view, the agents are doing a sort of oscillatory movement, going up and down in the water column. This is 
probably due to the fact that different layers of depth present different salinity distributions, and so the agents may be induced to follow the 
gradient of salinity in a vertical direction, instead of staying at the target depth.

From this figure, it is clear that the agents are trying to reach the target area, probably by following the filaments in the salinity field,
but that they don't know where the target area is. As before, they are oscillating in the vertical direction, but this time they seem to stay

The developed policies create non-intuitive behaviors, which are difficult to understand if the whole environment is not visible.
In fact, being it complex and made of currents, agents have developed a strategy that seems like following the salinity filaments,
which are zones in the environment that contain more or less the same salinity value, and the target area should be located in one of them. 

The results on the test set are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:success_matrix}. As can be seen, the success rate is higher when the agents 
are randomly initialized in the environment, which is the same scenario they were trained on, and when they are initialized uniformly 
in the spawning area. Moreover, when the target area is small, i.e. when the sampled point is rejected until the salinity value
is in the extreme $5\%$ of its distribution at that depth layer, the success rate is lower, since it is a more difficult scenario. 
All the policies perform better than a random agent, even though in certain cases like spawning at the origin the success rate is comparable to the random agent.

Overall, IPPO without communication performs better with 4 agents, while MAPPO is comparable to it with 2 agents. This shows 
that the use of a centralized critic is not always beneficial, and that independent agents in this task are an optimal solution.
The use of communication, instead, is beneficial in the harder scenarios and when agents are distributed in the environment.
