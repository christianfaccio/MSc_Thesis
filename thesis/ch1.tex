\chapter{Introduction}

Coastal regions are home to a significant portion of the global population,
and they are increasingly affected by human activities. Desalination processes \cite{omerspahic2022brine} have
become the main solution to provide freshwater to many of these regions, and even though
more efficient techniques have been developed, the accompanying environmental issues have 
been overlooked, in particular the consequences of the discharge of desalination 
by-products, such as the effects of concentrate effluent on the marine coastal ecosystem. 
Moreover, other industrial activities, such as offshore oil and gas operations \cite{cordes2016oilgas}, 
have been shown to have a significant impact on the marine environment, including the
release of pollutants and the disruption of marine habitats. In fact, with the depletion of easily accessible
oil and gas resources, offshore drilling is moving into deeper waters and more sensitive areas,
without being matched by legislation that reflects modern practices of environmental conservation. 
Such regulations may help to increase environmental monitoring and protect sensitive marine areas and species. 
Therefore, the preservation and monitoring of marine environments is a strategic priority for coastal regions, 
especially in the Arabian Gulf, where the combination of high salinity, warm temperatures, and human activities 
poses a significant threat to marine ecosystems \cite{sheppard2010gulf}.

The Gulf's marine communities are additionally exposed to decades of accelerating coastal urbanization, identified as the single largest anthropogenic stressor acting on the region, ahead of oil and gas extraction and desalination combined \cite{sale2011gulf}, with land reclamation and dredging generating persistent turbidity plumes and direct habitat loss along the coastlines of Bahrain \cite{zainal2012reclamation} and the wider Arabian Gulf \cite{burt2014urbanization}. Desalination brine discharge, while its impact on basin-scale salinity remains modest and concentrated near coastal outfalls rather than spread evenly across the basin \cite{paparella2022salinity}, combines with recurring thermal stress to drive bleaching events on the last remaining reefs of the region \cite{hadjhammou2025bleaching}. These compounding, spatially localized pressures are precisely why salinity and turbidity, the two variables this thesis tasks the swarm with locating, are useful proxies for anthropogenic disturbance rather than arbitrary target quantities.

Coral reefs are one of the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, providing an existential habitat for a wide range of marine species,
and offering shelter, food and breeding ground. Worldwide, billions of people depend more or less
directly on coral reefs for a significant portion of their livelihoods, including fishing, tourism and coastal protection.
Even if not directly sustaining human life in many wealthier regions of the globe, coral reefs
provide an enormous economic value. Clearly, they are essential for the health of the marine environment
and the well-being of human societies, and should then be preserved and protected \cite{erftemeijer2012environmental}.
Unfortunately, they are highly sensitive
to environmental changes, and their degradation could have severe consequences for the 
environment and the economy. Coral preservation is the best way to protect them, but is not always
a viable option. Therefore, coral restoration is advocated as a promising tool to reduce coral
loss and restore damaged reefs, but active interventions are needed to avoid the collapse of coral reefs 
worldwide in the next decades \cite{burdett2024restoration}. Many restoration techniques have been developed and many 
studies have been conducted to evaluate their effectiveness, but the success of these methods
is still limited \cite{mula2025restoration}. $30-40\%$ of projects fail due to poor planning, 
unrealistic objectives, inadequate regular maintenance and persistent anthropogenic pressures. 
Moreover, restoration sites are often clustered around human settlements, which are not always 
the most suitable locations for coral growth, but limits human hazards and encourages safety 
for divers, while also reducing costs. 
While the ultimate goal of coral restoration is to return ecosystems to pre-disturbance states, achieving this goal 
is becoming increasingly difficult due to the complex and dynamic nature of marine environments, as well as the
increasingly limited availability of suitable restoration sites. 
Finally, the search for suitable restoration sites and the further monitoring of the marine environment health is 
a time-consuming and dangerous task for human divers, who are exposed to different hazards and provide
limited spatial coverage.

Framing restoration as a site-selection problem, rather than a purely biological one, has gained traction in the recent literature. Suitable habitat for coral growth can be characterized by tolerance windows over a handful of measurable environmental variables, for instance salinity between $28.7$ and $40.4$ PSU and temperature between $21.7$ and $29.6$ °C \cite{guan2015suitable}, which reduces the search for a restoration site to the search for a region of the water column satisfying a target band on quantities that can, in principle, be sensed directly. Remote sensing techniques can narrow down candidate sites at a coarse spatial scale, but cannot substitute for in situ assessment, since fine-grained, near-bottom conditions are what ultimately determine suitability \cite{schill2021siteselection}. Coupling hydrodynamic modeling with ecological risk assessment has similarly been proposed to steer artificial reef deployment away from coastal pollution sources \cite{nasseridoust2024artificialreef}, following the same criterion adopted in this thesis, where target zones are defined to avoid regions of anomalous salinity associated with discharge.

For these reasons, the use of autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs) has been proposed as a solution
to overcome the limitations of human divers and to increase safety and efficiency in the search task. 
In fact, with the recent technological advancements, swarms of underwater robots could provide
seamless and continuous monitoring and spatial search capabilities, while also reducing costs. 
They are able to collect data from the environment, like temperature, salinity and turbidity,
which are critical to assess habitat suitability for coral restoration. 
However, the use of AUVs in marine environments is still a challenging task, due to the complex and dynamic nature of the environment, as well as 
the limitations of current technologies. In fact, AUVs struggle in comunicating with each other, due 
to the absense of advanced technologies for underwater communication. BlueME \cite{talebi2026blueme} is compact magnetoelectric antenna array system
for underwater robot-to-robot communication, which is able to provide a reliable signal transmission
at distances beyond 700 meters while consuming less than 10 W of power. It demonstrates that communication 
in the kilometer range is possible, but the search area usually exceeds this range.
Moreover, the robot usually can not rely on GPS signals, which vanish rapidly underwater, and the use of 
cameras is limited by the low visibility.

The case for deploying multiple robots rather than a single AUV rests on a coverage and observability argument: many cheap, decentralized units relying on local communication can sweep a search area far faster and with far more redundancy against individual failures than a single, more capable vehicle operating alone \cite{duarte2016swarm}, a principle that has motivated recent work coupling swarm intelligence with digital twins for marine ecological monitoring \cite{couceiro2026paradigm} and bio-inspired collectives that coordinate three-dimensional behaviors using only implicit, locally sensed cues \cite{berlinger2021blueswarm}. The underlying task, locating a region of the water column that matches an unknown target signature, also falls within the broader class of adaptive sampling and source-seeking problems, addressed for single vehicles tracking river plume fronts \cite{berild2024adaptive}, sampling static marine fields \cite{stankiewicz2021adaptive}, and tracing chemical plumes to their source \cite{farrell2005cpt}. Extending this task class to a swarm setting, where a target must be found cooperatively rather than by a lone vehicle, is precisely the gap this thesis addresses.

Given the unknown and complex nature of the marine environment, AUVs need to be equipped with
advanced sensors and algorithms to navigate and map the environment. To this end, Multi-agent 
Reinforcement Learning (MARL) algorithms have been recently proposed and increasingly used to overcome
this task, enhancing the capabilities of Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithms with swarm and cooperation 
capabilities, so that multiple agents can work together to achieve a common goal. A previous thesis \cite{insaghi2025thesis}
developed and analyzed a PPO-PSO algorithm to efficiently use swarm capabilities to overcome 
local optima and improve the performance of the PPO algorithm. The results showed that the PPO-PSO 
algorithm outperformed the standard PPO algorithm in terms of convergence speed and final performance, 
demonstrating the potential of MARL algorithms for complex tasks in unknown environments. However,
the environment used in the previous thesis was a simplified simulation, and the necessity to test 
the algorithm in a more realistic environment is the main motivation for this thesis. Therefore,
the main objective of this thesis is to evaluate the performance of a suitable MARL algorithm in a 
more realistic environment, where the agents are required to navigate and search for suitable 
coral restoration sites in a complex and unknown environment.

Closer still to this thesis, the same research group has applied multi-agent reinforcement learning to underwater monitoring under environmental uncertainty, showing the benefits of learned collaboration over independent policies \cite{luvisutto2025marl}, while cooperative control for multi-AUV systems via MARL has recently been surveyed as a fast-growing subfield spanning centralized-training decentralized-execution methods, communication-augmented policies and hybrid metaheuristic approaches \cite{marlsurvey2025multiauv}.

This thesis is organized as follows: Chapter 2 provides a comprehensive theoretical background on the 
Reinforcement Learning algorithms, including the multi-agent extension and general problem formulation. Chapter 3 
describes the problem more specifically, including the assumptions, constraints and tools used. Chapter 4 
introduces the proposed MARL algorithm, including its architecture, hyperparameters and implementation details.
Chapter 5 presents the experimental setup, where multiple scenarios are created to test the capabilities 
of the obtained policy and its generalization capabilities. Finally, Chapter 6 concludes the thesis,
summarizing the main findings, discussing the limitations and proposing future research directions.
