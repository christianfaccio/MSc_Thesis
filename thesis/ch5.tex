\chapter{Experiments}

A set of experiments was designed to evaluate the performance and usefulness of the components of the proposed framework. 
For example, the effect of communication and CTDE on top of independent agents has not been yet evaluated,
the increase of the number of agents seems not to bring any benefit except more coverage, and different scenarios 
have not been tested. Thus, in this chapter, the results and relative analysis of the experiments are presented.

Experiments are made of a set of 100 episodes each, and in each experiment some parameters are modified so that 
their effect can be evaluated. These parameters are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Communication radius: $\left\{\infty, 250, 0\right\}$ meters. This tests if communication is effectively used by the agents or if they perform better without it.
    \item Target area selection: $\left\{random,tail\right\}$. When selected randomly, the target values are taken from a randomly
    sampled point in the environment, which could lead to a target area that is quite large. When selected from the tail of the salinity distribution at that depth layer, 
    the target area is definately smaller, and the task is more difficult;
    \item Agent initialization: $\left\{random,origin,max\_dist\right\}$. When initialized randomly, each agent spawns in a random point of the environment. When initialized 
    at the origin, each agent spawns in the same point ($0,0$). When initialized with a maximum distance, the agents are spawned uniformly in the spawning area, which is defined by the south and west boundaries at surface level.
\end{itemize}

In the following sections, each experiment is analyzed carefully.

\section{Communication and CTDE}

Prior to any experiment, one can hypothesize that communication and CTDE, being them two ways of letting the agents share information, should help 
with difficult targets and have a more pronounced effect when the number of agents is increased. In Figure~\ref{fig:mech_effect}, it is possible to 
notice that communication sort of follows this idea, since it has a higher effect with a larger number of agents, and in general the effect is more
pronounced with the harder targets. When targets are sampled randomly, there is not much evidence of communication effectiveness, but when target areas 
are smaller, communication helps achieving success rates even $24\%$ higher than the relative baseline. MAPPO seems to benefit from communication when 
more agents are present, while IPPO benefits from it when the target areas are small. Regarding CTDE, it seems that it hurts more then it helps. In fact,
it seems to bring benefits only in the cases where agents don't have communication, signalling that the two mechanisms are complementary and should not be used together.
Also, it seems that CTDE is more effective when the number of agents is small, and when they are initialized either in the same point of uniformly in the spawning area.

    \label{fig:mech_effect}
\end{figure}

\section{Agent initialization}

How agents are initialized in the environment matters. Not only because they are able to cover more area but also because of the effect of communication.
Figure~\ref{fig:agent_init_effect} shows the success rate and the time to success for the different initialization methods that were tested.
In general, the best results are obtained when agents are initialized randomly in the environment, since they were trained on that scenario to avoid introducing bias.
The other two methods are more realistic, since they simulate the release of real AUVs from the land. The origin initialization, 
which spawns all the agents at the same point, is the worst case scenario in all cases. This is because the agents, made of the same policy, will tend to act 
the same way under the same circumstances, and if they start at the same point, they are in similar states from the beginning. That way, they cover less
area and the effect of communication is less pronounced since they can not know the target values at distant points in space, but are limited to close points' values.
The uniform initialization at the land boundaries is a better case, and it sort of reflects the random initialization, since results are similar. 
In this case, agents are naturally spread in the environment, and they can communicate to the others the target values they find, which helps 
the peers to know better about the environment even at distant points.

The shadowed lines are the marginalization of the bold ones on the two target methods, where the upper ones refer to random targets.}

\section{Difficult targets}

Since in this task the target to search for is an area and not a point, and it has been shown that on average it is quite large,
what happens if a smaller area is used as a target? To carry on this experiment, a random point is sampled in the environment until
the salinity value is in the extreme $5\%$ of its distribution at that depth layer. As Figure~\ref{fig:difficult_targets} shows, the success rate
is naturally lower, but communication and CTDE help in these cases. In the figure, the plots show the fraction of episodes solved
in function of the step budget, with 2 and 4 agents, in the random and tail targets. In the upper left plot, it is noticeable that
the policies with communication or CTDE perform better than independent agents, which is not the case with random sampled targets. 
In the 4 agents case, this does not hold, since here independent agents are the optimal choice. As said before, this is probably a natural 
training limitation of this task, where the use of large zones as targets limit the learning of cooperation and optimal policies as the number of agents is increased,
holding the environment fixed. Moreover, the effect of communication and CTDE is more pronounced with a higher step budget, i.e., when agents
have more time to explore and understand the environment for the search task. This is easily noticeable in the case of difficult targets, since
the line plotted does not have a linear trend as shown in the random targets' ones.

\section{Communication range}

Agents were trained with an infinite value for the communication range either for simplicity and for the small environment domain,
for which is assumed to exist a technology that let the agents communicate some bits for the targets' values and relative distance
to each other. However, it is not said that communication is effectively used by the agents, and this experiment is made exactly for this 
reason. 

As Figure~\ref{fig:communication_range} shows, with 2 agents the communication is effectively used, since the success rate drops as the 
communication range is cut. This indicates that the agents are able to use the advantage given by knowing the relative position of the other
teammates and the target values they register to increase the success rate of a mission. 
However, using 4 agents it is the opposite, i.e., they are better without communication, and this has been 
extensively seen before, as independent agents in this case were the best option, and here is definitely confirmed.
In fact, training a policy with 4 agents is limited by the nature of the problem, which therefore limits the learning of cooperation and communication, since the agents are trained on large target areas, which are easy to find.

\section{Scaling N}

When increasing the number of agents, the first thing that comes to mind is that the success rate should increase just because there are more agents to cover the area. 
However, it may not be the case for all the policies, due to the effect of communication and CTDE. 

In Figure~\ref{fig:scaling_n}, it is possible to see the success rate 
and the penalized time to success as a function of the number of agents. The penalized time to success 
is computed as average time of the minimum between the time to success and the maximum number of steps at each episode, which is a metric that takes into account the number of episodes solved, since if an agent does not succeed in an episode, it is penalized with the maximum number of steps.
In general, the success rate increases as the number of agents increases, and the time to success
descreases as the number of agents increases, as one expects. The only exception is in this case MAPPO without communication, that seems to be worse with respect to all other policies.
It has to be said, however, that this plot only considers the average value computed on all the scenarios, and is, therefore, a general metric. 
Previous figures, in fact, showed the effect of changing the number of agents in specific cases. For example, the use of communication on MAPPO was 
more effective on 4 agents than on 2 agents, as showed in Figure~\ref{fig:mech_effect}. At the end, however, all comes down to the limitation brought by the nature of the problem, i.e., the use of large target areas, which limited the learning
of the policy with 4 agents and the subsequent use of communication and CTDE. More variables should be considered to solve this limitation.

\section{Time to success}

The success rate is not the only metric that can be used to evaluate the performance of a policy. 
In fact, it does not consider the time it takes for an agent to find the target zone. An agent that 
always succeeds but takes a long time to do so may be less useful than an agent that succeeds less often
but finds the target quickly, since batteries don't have infinite capacity. Therefore, a thorough analysis
on the times to reach the target is necessary. Figure ~\ref{fig:time_to_first_success} shows on the left charts the median time
to reach target for the first agent only, while on the right charts the same metric but weighted for the 
number of episodes solved. 

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{assets/fig5_time_to_first_success.pdf}
    \caption{Median times to succeed for the first agent only (left column) and the relative normalized metric (right column), which is computed as $1-\mathbb{E}(\min (t,T))/T$, where $T$ refers to the \textit{max\_steps} parameter. The first row refers to 2 agents' cases, while the second row to 4 agents' ones.}
    \label{fig:time_to_first_success}
\end{figure}

As can be noticed, the faster times can be found in the scenarios with random sampled targets and randomly initialized agents, the one the policies are trained on. 
The cases where targets are harder take longer to be solved, and the effect of communication in those cases is helpful, since it reduces the time to success.
In the plot with 4 agents, there are larger times, but as can be seen in the right plot, weighted for the number of solved episodes the metric results in a 
value comparable to the one with 2 agents. In fact, the median time is higher because more difficult episodes are solved. 

Scenarios where success is determined by all agents reaching the target have also been considered. However, training on those specific scenarios is not trivial,
since the reward must be carefully designed, since it is shared and some agents may not reach target. Therefore, the trained policies were evaluated on those
cases, and the results are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:arrival_by_rank_N2} for 2 agents' policies and in Figure~\ref{fig:arrival_by_rank_N4} for 4 agents' policies. 
The figures show the probability of reaching target based on the rank of the agent, as a function of the step budget.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{assets/fig6_arrival_by_rank_N2.pdf}
    \caption{Probabilities to arrive at target by function of the number of steps for the first (left column) and second (right column) agent for the policies trained with 2 agents. The first row refers to cases where agents are initialized uniformly at land, the second one to cases where agents are initialized at the environment origin $(0,0)$.}
    \label{fig:arrival_by_rank_N2}
\end{figure}

For 2 agents, the second agent reaches target with a lower probability than the first one, as expected. Moreover,
the policies that results in better success rates are the ones that use either communication or CTDE. With an initialization 
of the agents in the same point, the metric is higher at lower budgets, but with uniform initialization the results are more robust
between the policies.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{assets/fig6_arrival_by_rank_N4.pdf}
    \caption{Probabilities to arrive at target by function of the number of steps for the first (first column), second (second column), third (third column) and fourth (fourth column) agent for the policies trained with 4 agents. The first row refers to cases where agents are initialized uniformly at land, the second one to cases where agents are initialized at the environment origin $(0,0)$.}
    \label{fig:arrival_by_rank_N4}
\end{figure}

Regarding the 4 agents' policies, the results are more interesting. In fact, MAPPO with communication seems to perform better than 
all the other policies, in each case, and it is more pronounced in third and fourth agents with initialization at the same point. MAPPO 
alone here performs poorly, showing that communication helps later agents to understand that they need to go where the agents that already 
succeeded are.
